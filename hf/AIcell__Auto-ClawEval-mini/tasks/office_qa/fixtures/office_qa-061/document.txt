UNITED STATES DEPARTMENT OF DEFENSE
OFFICE OF THE UNDER SECRETARY OF DEFENSE (COMPTROLLER)
FISCAL YEAR 2024 DEFENSE SPENDING ANALYSIS
Published: February 14, 2025

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report presents a comprehensive analysis of United States defense expenditures for Fiscal Year 2024 (October 1, 2023 through September 30, 2024), including comparisons to prior fiscal years, breakdowns by appropriation category, and projections for FY2025 and FY2026. The analysis draws on data certified by the Defense Finance and Accounting Service (DFAS) and reviewed by the Government Accountability Office (GAO).

Total obligated defense spending for FY2024 reached $886.3 billion, representing an increase of 4.7 percent over the FY2023 enacted level of $847.3 billion. This figure encompasses both the base budget of $849.8 billion and $36.5 billion in supplemental appropriations, the majority of which were directed toward security assistance to Ukraine and Indo-Pacific deterrence initiatives.

---

SECTION 1: APPROPRIATIONS OVERVIEW

The FY2024 National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA), signed into law by President Biden on December 22, 2023, authorized $886.3 billion in total defense spending. This represents the largest defense budget in nominal terms in United States history, surpassing the previous record of $858 billion authorized in FY2023.

SECTION 2: SERVICE-BY-SERVICE BREAKDOWN

The Department of the Army received $185.7 billion in FY2024, an increase of 5.2 percent from its FY2023 allocation of $176.5 billion. This growth was driven primarily by increased procurement of Patriot missile defense systems and expanded funding for the Multi-Domain Task Force program.

The Department of the Navy, including the Marine Corps, received $257.3 billion, up 3.9 percent from $247.6 billion in FY2023. Shipbuilding accounts represented the largest single growth area, with $32.8 billion allocated to the Shipbuilding and Conversion, Navy (SCN) account — a 6.4 percent increase over FY2023's $30.8 billion. The Virginia-class submarine program received $9.1 billion, funding the procurement of two boats in FY2024.

The Department of the Air Force, which includes the Space Force, received $268.1 billion, representing a 5.8 percent increase over FY2023's $253.4 billion. The F-35A procurement program accounted for $4.7 billion of this total, funding 48 aircraft. The Space Force component received $29.4 billion, a 12.3 percent increase reflecting accelerated investment in satellite communications and space domain awareness capabilities.

Defense-wide agencies and programs, including the Defense Intelligence Agency, Defense Logistics Agency, and Missile Defense Agency, collectively received $175.2 billion.

---

SECTION 3: RESEARCH, DEVELOPMENT, TEST AND EVALUATION

RDT&E funding at $143.2 billion represents the fastest-growing major appropriation category in FY2024, increasing 10.1 percent year-over-year. The following programs received the largest individual RDT&E allocations:

- B-21 Raider bomber program (Northrop Grumman): $3.8 billion
- Next Generation Air Dominance (NGAD) fighter program: $1.9 billion
- Hypersonic weapons development (multiple contractors): $4.7 billion
- Artificial intelligence and autonomous systems: $2.3 billion
- Columbia-class ballistic missile submarine: $2.1 billion
- Sentinel intercontinental ballistic missile (Northrop Grumman): $3.4 billion

The Sentinel ICBM program, which is replacing the aging Minuteman III, has experienced significant cost growth. The program's acquisition cost estimate was revised upward in January 2024 to $131 billion, a 37 percent increase over the 2020 baseline estimate of $95.8 billion. This cost growth triggered a Nunn-McCurdy breach notification, requiring the Secretary of Defense to certify the program's continued necessity — a certification provided by Secretary Lloyd Austin on March 15, 2024.

---

SECTION 4: OVERSEAS CONTINGENCY OPERATIONS AND SUPPLEMENTAL FUNDING

The FY2024 supplemental appropriations package, passed by Congress on April 23, 2024, provided $95.3 billion in emergency funding, of which $60.8 billion was directed to Ukraine Security Assistance. Of the Ukraine-related funding, $23.2 billion was designated for direct military assistance including artillery ammunition, air defense interceptors, and armored vehicles. An additional $14.4 billion was allocated for replenishment of U.S. stockpiles drawn down through Presidential Drawdown Authority transfers.

Israel received $14.1 billion in security assistance through the same supplemental package, including $4.0 billion for Iron Dome and David's Sling interceptor replenishment and $3.5 billion in Foreign Military Financing grants.

Indo-Pacific deterrence initiatives received $8.1 billion, funding enhanced rotational presence in the Philippines, Guam infrastructure improvements, and pre-positioning of munitions in allied nations.

---

SECTION 5: PERSONNEL COSTS AND END STRENGTH

Military Personnel appropriations of $183.7 billion funded an active-duty end strength of 1,328,500 personnel as of September 30, 2024, a decrease of 11,200 from the FY2023 authorized end strength of 1,339,700. Recruiting shortfalls, particularly in the Army, which fell 10,000 personnel short of its FY2023 recruiting goal, contributed to this reduction.

Average annual compensation per active-duty service member, including basic pay, housing allowance, subsistence allowance, and tax advantages, reached $138,400 in FY2024. The FY2024 NDAA authorized a 5.2 percent across-the-board pay increase for military personnel, the largest such increase since 2002.

Reserve component personnel costs totaled $47.3 billion, supporting a Selected Reserve end strength of 798,500.

Civilian defense workforce compensation totaled $88.6 billion, covering approximately 762,000 full-time equivalent civilian employees across the Department of Defense.

---

SECTION 6: OPERATIONS AND MAINTENANCE TRENDS

The Operations and Maintenance (O&M) account at $325.1 billion funds day-to-day readiness, training, base operations, and depot maintenance. Key observations include:

- Depot maintenance funding reached $26.8 billion, still approximately $4.2 billion below the statutory requirement that 50 percent of depot workload be performed at government-owned facilities.
- Flying hours funding supported 1.24 million total flight hours across all services, a 3.1 percent increase over FY2023.
- Fuel costs totaled $17.3 billion, reflecting an average price of $3.84 per gallon for aviation fuel under the Defense Logistics Agency's standard price mechanism.
- Base operations support funding of $22.4 billion maintained 514 installations worldwide.

---

SECTION 7: DEFENSE SPENDING IN INTERNATIONAL CONTEXT

U.S. defense spending in FY2024 represented approximately 3.4 percent of Gross Domestic Product, based on the Bureau of Economic Analysis's advance estimate of FY2024 GDP at $28.3 trillion. This percentage remains below the Cold War peak of 6.2 percent reached in 1986 but above the post-Cold War trough of 2.9 percent recorded in 2000.

Among NATO allies, the United States accounted for approximately 68 percent of total alliance defense spending in 2024. Twenty-three of thirty-two NATO member nations met or exceeded the 2 percent of GDP defense spending target in 2024, compared to eleven nations in 2023.

China's officially reported defense budget for 2024 was 1.6655 trillion yuan (approximately $231 billion at prevailing exchange rates), representing a 7.2 percent nominal increase over 2023. U.S. defense analysts, including the Office of the Secretary of Defense's annual China Military Power report, estimate actual Chinese defense-related spending at between $330 billion and $400 billion when accounting for items excluded from official figures.

---

SECTION 8: OUTLOOK FOR FY2025 AND FY2026

The President's Budget Request for FY2025, submitted to Congress on March 11, 2024, proposed $849.8 billion in base defense spending plus anticipated supplemental requirements. The FY2025 NDAA, signed December 23, 2024, authorized $895.2 billion, a 1.0 percent real increase after accounting for a defense inflation rate of 3.6 percent as measured by the Office of the Under Secretary of Defense (Comptroller).

The Future Years Defense Program (FYDP) projects cumulative defense spending of approximately $4.7 trillion over the FY2025 through FY2029 period, assuming continued annual real growth of approximately 1 percent above inflation.

---

CONCLUSION

FY2024 defense spending reflects sustained investment in modernization priorities, particularly in RDT&E and procurement, while managing persistent cost growth challenges in major acquisition programs. The Sentinel ICBM cost breach, shipbuilding delays, and recruiting shortfalls represent the most significant programmatic risks requiring continued oversight. The Office of the Under Secretary of Defense (Comptroller) will publish the FY2024 Agency Financial Report no later than November 15, 2025.
