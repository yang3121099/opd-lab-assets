OFFICE OF MANAGEMENT AND BUDGET
FISCAL YEAR 2024 DEFENSE SPENDING ANALYSIS
Prepared by the Budget Analysis Division | Released March 15, 2025

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The Department of Defense received a total appropriation of $886.3 billion for Fiscal Year 2024, representing a 3.2 percent increase over the FY2023 enacted level of $858.5 billion. This report examines allocation patterns, year-over-year growth trends, personnel costs, procurement expenditures, and research and development investments across all major defense components. The analysis draws on data submitted by the Office of the Secretary of Defense, the Joint Chiefs of Staff, and the individual military service branches through the Defense Finance and Accounting Service (DFAS) as of December 31, 2024.

SECTION 1: OVERALL BUDGET OVERVIEW

The FY2024 National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA), signed into law by President Biden on December 22, 2023, authorized $886.3 billion in total defense spending. This figure encompasses both discretionary and mandatory spending components and represents approximately 13.1 percent of total federal outlays for FY2024, which stood at $6.75 trillion.

Defense spending as a share of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) held at 3.1 percent in FY2024, consistent with the FY2023 figure and slightly above the 2.9 percent recorded in FY2022. The United States remains the world's largest defense spender, accounting for approximately 37 percent of total global military expenditures, according to the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI) 2024 report.

Five-Year Defense Spending Trend (in billions USD):

FY2020: $738.0
FY2021: $753.5
FY2022: $782.0
FY2023: $858.5
FY2024: $886.3

Compound annual growth rate (CAGR) from FY2020 to FY2024: 4.7 percent

SECTION 2: ALLOCATION BY MILITARY BRANCH AND MAJOR COMPONENT

The FY2024 appropriation was distributed across the following primary accounts:

Department of the Army: $185.4 billion (20.9% of total)
Department of the Navy (including Marine Corps): $230.8 billion (26.1%)
Department of the Air Force (including Space Force): $231.6 billion (26.1%)
Defense-Wide Activities: $145.7 billion (16.4%)
Military Construction and Family Housing: $18.3 billion (2.1%)
Defense Health Program: $57.2 billion (6.5%)
Other Defense Programs: $17.3 billion (2.0%)

The Air Force and Navy tied as the largest single-branch recipients, each receiving approximately $231 billion. The Defense-Wide Activities account, which funds agencies such as the Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA), the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA), and the Missile Defense Agency (MDA), grew by 6.8 percent compared to FY2023, the highest growth rate among major accounts.

SECTION 3: PERSONNEL COSTS

Military personnel costs totaled $176.2 billion in FY2024, representing 19.9 percent of the total defense budget. This reflects a 5.5 percent increase over the FY2023 personnel cost of $167.0 billion, driven primarily by a 5.2 percent across-the-board pay raise for active-duty service members that took effect January 1, 2024 — the largest military pay increase in more than two decades.

Active-duty end strength as of September 30, 2024 stood at 1,328,400 personnel, a slight decrease of 0.4 percent from the FY2023 figure of 1,333,700. Recruitment shortfalls, particularly in the Army, which missed its FY2023 recruiting goal by approximately 15,000 soldiers, continued to affect total force size.

Civilian defense workforce costs reached $84.6 billion, covering approximately 762,000 civilian employees across all DoD components. The Defense Health Program accounted for $57.2 billion, serving approximately 9.6 million TRICARE beneficiaries including active-duty members, retirees, and their dependents.

Reserve and National Guard personnel costs totaled $47.8 billion, reflecting increased operational tempo and ongoing deployments in support of European Command (EUCOM) operations related to security assistance to Ukraine.

SECTION 4: PROCUREMENT EXPENDITURES

Total procurement spending in FY2024 reached $167.4 billion, a 4.1 percent increase from the FY2023 level of $160.8 billion. Major procurement categories included:

Aircraft Procurement: $52.3 billion
  - F-35 Joint Strike Fighter program: $15.6 billion (covering 83 aircraft across all variants)
  - B-21 Raider bomber: $2.8 billion
  - MH-60R/S Seahawk helicopters: $1.4 billion

Shipbuilding and Conversion, Navy: $32.7 billion
  - Virginia-class submarine program: $9.8 billion (2 boats funded)
  - Gerald R. Ford-class carrier (CVN-82): $1.2 billion (advance procurement)
  - Constellation-class frigate (FFG-62): $1.6 billion

Missile and Munitions Procurement: $29.8 billion
  - Patriot Advanced Capability-3 (PAC-3) missiles: $2.1 billion
  - Javelin anti-tank missiles: $1.3 billion
  - Tomahawk cruise missiles: $1.1 billion
  - Long-Range Anti-Ship Missile (LRASM): $0.8 billion

Ground Vehicle Procurement: $14.6 billion
  - Armored Multi-Purpose Vehicle (AMPV): $1.2 billion
  - M1A2 SEPv3 Abrams tank upgrades: $0.9 billion

Other Procurement (equipment, communications, IT systems): $38.0 billion

The F-35 program, managed by Lockheed Martin and administered through the F-35 Joint Program Office at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base, Ohio, remained the single largest procurement line item for the sixth consecutive year.

SECTION 5: RESEARCH, DEVELOPMENT, TEST, AND EVALUATION (RDT&E)

RDT&E funding totaled $145.0 billion in FY2024, an increase of 6.2 percent from the FY2023 level of $136.5 billion. This category represents the fastest-growing major spending account over the five-year period from FY2020 to FY2024, with a CAGR of 8.3 percent.

Key RDT&E investments included:

Hypersonic Weapons Development: $4.7 billion across Army, Navy, and Air Force programs, including the Conventional Prompt Strike (CPS) and Air-Launched Rapid Response Weapon (ARRW) programs.

Artificial Intelligence and Autonomous Systems: $3.1 billion, coordinated through the Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Office (CDAO), established in February 2022.

Directed Energy Weapons: $1.2 billion, including high-energy laser programs managed by the Air Force Research Laboratory (AFRL) at Kirtland Air Force Base, New Mexico.

Space Systems Development: $11.4 billion, including funding for the Next Generation Overhead Persistent Infrared (Next Gen OPIR) satellite constellation and Space Development Agency (SDA) proliferated low-Earth orbit (pLEO) satellite programs.

Nuclear Modernization (DoD portion): $18.3 billion, supporting the Columbia-class ballistic missile submarine, the B-21 Raider, and the Sentinel intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM) program, which replaced the aging Minuteman III system.

DARPA received $4.1 billion in FY2024, a 7.9 percent increase over its FY2023 budget of $3.8 billion, funding programs in biotechnology, quantum computing, and advanced materials.

SECTION 6: OPERATION AND MAINTENANCE

Operation and Maintenance (O&M) funding totaled $310.4 billion, the single largest spending category at 35.0 percent of the total defense budget. O&M covers day-to-day operating costs including training, base operations, equipment maintenance, and logistics.

Notable O&M expenditures included:
- Ukraine Security Assistance Initiative: $7.8 billion
- Indo-Pacific deterrence activities: $9.1 billion
- Overseas Contingency Operations (residual): $3.2 billion

SECTION 7: COMPARATIVE INTERNATIONAL CONTEXT

For context, the following represents estimated FY2024 defense expenditures by major nations (in USD billions, converted at current exchange rates, sourced from SIPRI and NATO estimates):

United States: $886.3
China (estimated): $296.4
Russia (estimated): $109.0
India: $83.6
Saudi Arabia: $75.8
United Kingdom: $74.9
Germany: $66.8

The United States outspent the next nine largest defense budgets combined. NATO's collective defense spending, excluding the United States, reached approximately $380 billion in FY2024, with 23 of 32 NATO member nations meeting or exceeding the alliance's 2 percent of GDP spending target — up from 11 nations in 2023.

SECTION 8: AUDIT STATUS AND FINANCIAL MANAGEMENT

The Department of Defense received its seventh consecutive disclaimer of opinion on its agency-wide financial statements from the DoD Inspector General in November 2024. While the Department has made measurable progress — with the Army achieving a clean audit opinion on its General Fund for the first time in FY2024 — significant financial management weaknesses persist across the Navy and Defense Logistics Agency (DLA).

Deputy Secretary of Defense Kathleen Hicks directed the implementation of a revised Financial Improvement and Audit Remediation (FIAR) plan in January 2024, targeting a department-wide clean audit opinion by FY2028.

CONCLUSION

FY2024 defense spending of $886.3 billion reflects sustained investment in military readiness, modernization, and deterrence amid a complex global security environment. The 3.2 percent nominal increase, while below the rate of inflation in some procurement categories, maintained real purchasing power in priority areas including hypersonics, space, and nuclear modernization. Continued attention to audit compliance, recruiting challenges, and cost growth in major acquisition programs — particularly the Sentinel ICBM, which experienced a Nunn-McCurdy critical breach in January 2024 with unit cost growth exceeding 37 percent above baseline — will remain central concerns for Congressional oversight in FY2025.
