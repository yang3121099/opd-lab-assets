EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report provides a comprehensive analysis of United States defense spending for Fiscal Year 2024, including historical comparisons, program-level breakdowns, and forward projections through FY2028. Total national defense discretionary spending for FY2024 stands at $886.3 billion, representing a 3.2 percent increase over the FY2023 enacted level of $858.5 billion. This figure encompasses the Department of Defense base budget, Overseas Contingency Operations funding, and nuclear weapons activities administered by the Department of Energy.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

SECTION 1: OVERVIEW OF FY2024 DEFENSE APPROPRIATIONS

The National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2024, signed into law by President Biden on December 22, 2023, authorized $886.3 billion in total defense spending. This authorization reflects a $27.8 billion increase from the prior fiscal year and marks the largest single-year dollar increase in defense appropriations since FY2019.

The Department of Defense base budget accounts for $849.8 billion of total authorized spending. The remaining $36.5 billion is allocated to defense-related activities outside the DoD, primarily the National Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA) within the Department of Energy, which received $23.1 billion, and other defense-related agencies receiving the balance of $13.4 billion.

As a percentage of Gross Domestic Product, FY2024 defense spending represents approximately 3.1 percent, consistent with the prior year and slightly below the 10-year average of 3.4 percent recorded between FY2014 and FY2023.

SECTION 3: SERVICE-LEVEL ALLOCATIONS

The four military departments received the following base budget allocations for FY2024:

Department of the Army: $185.7 billion (21.8% of DoD base budget)
Department of the Navy (including Marine Corps): $230.8 billion (27.2% of DoD base budget)
Department of the Air Force (including Space Force): $258.4 billion (30.4% of DoD base budget)
Defense-Wide Agencies and Activities: $174.9 billion (20.6% of DoD base budget)

The Air Force and Space Force combined allocation represents the largest single-service share for the third consecutive fiscal year, driven primarily by continued investment in next-generation air superiority platforms and Space Force constellation expansion. Space Force alone received $29.4 billion in FY2024, up from $26.3 billion in FY2023, a year-over-year increase of 11.8 percent.

SECTION 4: MAJOR ACQUISITION PROGRAMS

The following programs represent the largest individual procurement line items in the FY2024 defense budget:

F-35 Lightning II Joint Strike Fighter Program
Contractor: Lockheed Martin Corporation
FY2024 Procurement Funding: $15.6 billion
Aircraft Procured: 83 aircraft across all variants (48 F-35A, 17 F-35B, 18 F-35C)
Cumulative Program Cost to Date: $412.7 billion
Projected Total Program Cost (lifecycle): $1.727 trillion

Columbia-Class Ballistic Missile Submarine
Contractor: General Dynamics Electric Boat (prime); Huntington Ingalls Industries (partner)
FY2024 Procurement Funding: $6.2 billion
Program Status: Lead boat USS Columbia (SSBN-826) under construction; delivery expected FY2030
Total Program Acquisition Cost: $109.8 billion for 12 submarines

B-21 Raider Long-Range Strike Bomber
Contractor: Northrop Grumman Corporation
FY2024 Research, Development, Test & Evaluation Funding: $2.9 billion
FY2024 Procurement Funding: $1.1 billion (advance procurement)
Initial Operational Capability Target: FY2026
Planned Fleet Size: Minimum 100 aircraft

Constellation-Class Guided Missile Frigate (FFG-62)
Contractor: Fincantieri Marinette Marine
FY2024 Procurement Funding: $1.4 billion (1 ship)
Total Ships Planned: 20
Unit Cost (current estimate): $1.28 billion per ship

SECTION 5: PERSONNEL AND COMPENSATION

Military personnel costs totaling $174.9 billion in FY2024 reflect a 5.2 percent basic pay increase effective January 1, 2024, the largest military pay raise since 2002. This increase was driven by the Employment Cost Index benchmark and congressional direction to improve military compensation competitiveness with the private sector.

Active duty end strength authorized for FY2024:

Army: 452,000 soldiers (down from 466,000 in FY2023)
Navy: 346,000 sailors (up from 342,000 in FY2023)
Marine Corps: 177,000 marines (unchanged from FY2023)
Air Force: 328,000 airmen (down from 333,000 in FY2023)
Space Force: 9,400 guardians (up from 8,600 in FY2023)
Total Active Duty: 1,312,400 (net decrease of 7,200 from FY2023)

Reserve component end strength totals 798,500 across all components, with the Army National Guard comprising the largest reserve element at 325,000 soldiers.

SECTION 6: RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT PRIORITIES

The $143.2 billion Research, Development, Test and Evaluation (RDT&E) appropriation for FY2024 represents a 10.1 percent increase over FY2023 and the highest RDT&E investment in inflation-adjusted terms since the peak of the Strategic Defense Initiative funding in the late 1980s.

Key technology investment areas include:

Artificial Intelligence and Autonomous Systems: $3.7 billion across all services and defense agencies, coordinated through the Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Office (CDAO) established in February 2022 under Deputy Secretary of Defense Kathleen Hicks.

Hypersonic Weapons Development: $4.7 billion total, including $1.9 billion for the Army's Long-Range Hypersonic Weapon (LRHW), $1.2 billion for the Navy's Conventional Prompt Strike (CPS) program, and $1.6 billion for Air Force hypersonic programs.

Directed Energy Weapons: $1.1 billion, with the High Energy Laser with Integrated Optical-dazzler and Surveillance (HELIOS) system aboard USS Preble (DDG-88) completing operational testing in November 2023.

Microelectronics and Trusted Foundry: $3.2 billion, reflecting implementation of the CHIPS and Science Act provisions affecting defense supply chains.

SECTION 8: INTERNATIONAL COMPARISONS

United States defense spending in FY2024 represents approximately 37 percent of total global military expenditure, based on Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI) preliminary estimates. The next largest defense budgets belong to China (estimated $225 billion), Russia (estimated $109 billion), India ($83.6 billion), Saudi Arabia ($75.8 billion), and the United Kingdom ($68.5 billion).

Among NATO allies, 23 of 31 member nations are projected to meet or exceed the alliance's 2 percent of GDP defense spending benchmark in 2024, compared to only 7 nations meeting the benchmark in 2014, the year the Wales Pledge was adopted.

SECTION 9: OVERSIGHT AND AUDIT STATUS

The Department of Defense has now completed six consecutive department-wide financial statement audits since the first full audit was conducted in FY2018. The FY2023 audit, completed November 15, 2023, resulted in a disclaimer of opinion for the seventh consecutive year, though auditors noted material weakness reductions in 11 of 28 identified categories. The DoD has invested $2.4 billion in audit remediation activities since FY2018. Deputy Secretary of Defense Kathleen Hicks has set a target of achieving a qualified audit opinion by FY2027.

CONCLUSION

FY2024 defense spending reflects continued prioritization of great power competition, nuclear modernization, and emerging technology investment. The 3.2 percent nominal increase maintains real purchasing power above inflation while sustaining commitments to allies and partners. The Congressional Budget Office projects that without legislative action to adjust discretionary spending caps, defense spending growth will moderate to approximately 2.9 percent annually through FY2028, potentially creating procurement shortfalls in the Columbia-class submarine and B-21 programs if cost growth continues at current rates.
