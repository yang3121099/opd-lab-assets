UNITED STATES DEPARTMENT OF DEFENSE
OFFICE OF THE UNDER SECRETARY OF DEFENSE (COMPTROLLER)
FISCAL YEAR 2024 DEFENSE SPENDING ANALYSIS
Published: February 14, 2025

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report presents a comprehensive analysis of United States defense expenditures for Fiscal Year 2024 (October 1, 2023 through September 30, 2024), including comparisons to prior fiscal years, breakdowns by appropriation category, and projections for FY2025 and FY2026. The analysis draws on data certified by the Defense Finance and Accounting Service (DFAS) and reviewed by the Government Accountability Office (GAO). Total obligated defense spending for FY2024 reached $886.4 billion, representing a 4.7 percent increase over the FY2023 enacted level of $847.3 billion.

SECTION 1: OVERVIEW OF FY2024 DEFENSE APPROPRIATIONS

The National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2024, signed into law by President Biden on December 22, 2023, authorized a total of $886.3 billion in defense-related spending. This figure encompasses both the base defense budget and Overseas Contingency Operations (OCO) funding, which was formally integrated into the base budget beginning in FY2022 following congressional direction.

The FY2024 defense budget represents 13.1 percent of total federal outlays, which stood at $6.75 trillion for the fiscal year. As a share of Gross Domestic Product, defense spending equaled approximately 3.1 percent, up slightly from 3.0 percent in FY2023. The United States remains the world's largest defense spender, accounting for approximately 37 percent of global military expenditures according to data published by the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI) in April 2024.

SECTION 2: SPENDING BY MAJOR APPROPRIATION CATEGORY

The following table summarizes FY2024 defense spending across the five primary appropriation titles:

Appropriation Category | FY2023 Enacted ($B) | FY2024 Enacted ($B) | Change (%)
Military Personnel | 176.2 | 183.7 | +4.3%
Operations and Maintenance | 298.6 | 314.9 | +5.5%
Procurement | 145.8 | 157.2 | +7.8%
Research, Development, Test & Evaluation | 130.1 | 143.8 | +10.5%
Military Construction & Family Housing | 18.4 | 19.6 | +6.5%
Other Defense Programs | 78.2 | 67.2 | -14.1%

Military Personnel costs at $183.7 billion reflect a 5.2 percent basic pay increase effective January 1, 2024, the largest such increase since 2002. This raise was implemented in response to recruiting shortfalls across all service branches, with the Army falling approximately 10,000 soldiers short of its FY2023 accession goal of 65,000.

Operations and Maintenance (O&M) funding at $314.9 billion constitutes the single largest appropriation category, representing 35.5 percent of total defense spending. Significant O&M cost drivers in FY2024 included increased fuel costs, depot maintenance backlogs, and expanded support to Ukraine under the Ukraine Security Assistance Initiative, which drew $7.8 billion in FY2024 alone.

SECTION 3: PROCUREMENT HIGHLIGHTS

Procurement funding of $157.2 billion in FY2024 supported major weapons system acquisitions across all service branches. Key procurement actions included:

- The Navy received $4.6 billion for two Virginia-class submarines (SSN-806 and SSN-807), continuing the Block VI production contract held by General Dynamics Electric Boat and Huntington Ingalls Industries.
- The Air Force obligated $11.3 billion for 48 F-35A aircraft under Lot 17 of the Joint Strike Fighter contract with Lockheed Martin Corporation, at a unit cost of approximately $82.5 million per aircraft.
- The Army funded 96 AH-64E Apache Guardian helicopters at a total cost of $2.1 billion under a contract with Boeing Defense, Space & Security.
- The Missile Defense Agency received $10.2 billion, including $1.4 billion for the Next Generation Interceptor program currently in development by Northrop Grumman and Raytheon Technologies.
- The Navy's Gerald R. Ford-class carrier program received $2.9 billion toward the construction of CVN-82, the future USS Enterprise, with delivery projected for FY2032.

SECTION 4: RESEARCH, DEVELOPMENT, TEST AND EVALUATION

RDT&E funding reached $143.8 billion in FY2024, a 10.5 percent increase over FY2023 and the highest level in inflation-adjusted terms since the early 1990s. This growth reflects the Department's prioritization of emerging technology competition with the People's Republic of China, as outlined in the 2022 National Defense Strategy.

Priority investment areas within RDT&E included:

- Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning: $3.7 billion across 685 individual programs, coordinated through the Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Office (CDAO) established in February 2022.
- Hypersonic Weapons: $4.7 billion, supporting programs including the Army's Long-Range Hypersonic Weapon (LRHW), the Navy's Conventional Prompt Strike (CPS), and the Air Force's AGM-183A Air-launched Rapid Response Weapon (ARRW), which was cancelled in March 2024 after failing to achieve required performance thresholds.
- Space Systems: $6.1 billion, including $2.3 billion for the Space Development Agency's Proliferated Warfighter Space Architecture (PWSA), targeting a constellation of 300 satellites by FY2026.
- Directed Energy Weapons: $1.2 billion, including continued development of the High Energy Laser with Integrated Optical-dazzler and Surveillance (HELIOS) system aboard USS Preble (DDG-88).

SECTION 5: GEOGRAPHIC AND COMMAND DISTRIBUTION

Defense spending obligations were distributed across combatant commands as follows for FY2024:

- U.S. Indo-Pacific Command (INDOPACOM): $61.4 billion, including $9.1 billion under the Pacific Deterrence Initiative (PDI) enacted in FY2021.
- U.S. European Command (EUCOM): $38.7 billion, reflecting continued elevated posture following Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. This represents a 22.3 percent increase over the pre-invasion FY2021 EUCOM baseline of $31.6 billion.
- U.S. Central Command (CENTCOM): $24.3 billion, down from $31.8 billion in FY2022 as force levels in the Middle East region declined.
- All other commands combined: $762.0 billion (primarily CONUS-based activities, training, and institutional functions).

SECTION 6: PERSONNEL AND WORKFORCE COSTS

As of September 30, 2024, the total active duty military end strength stood at 1,328,400 personnel, compared to an authorized end strength of 1,339,500. This shortfall of approximately 11,100 personnel represents the third consecutive year in which actual end strength fell below authorized levels.

Civilian defense workforce totaled 762,000 employees as of the same date, with an average annual compensation of $112,400 including benefits. The Defense Contract Audit Agency (DCAA) reported overseeing contracts valued at $487 billion during FY2024, with questioned costs totaling $8.3 billion across 1,247 audit reports.

SECTION 7: YEAR-OVER-YEAR TRENDS AND HISTORICAL CONTEXT

The following data points illustrate the trajectory of defense spending over the past decade:

FY2015: $560.4 billion
FY2016: $580.3 billion
FY2017: $598.5 billion
FY2018: $629.0 billion
FY2019: $685.0 billion
FY2020: $738.0 billion
FY2021: $753.0 billion
FY2022: $782.0 billion
FY2023: $847.3 billion
FY2024: $886.4 billion

This represents a cumulative increase of 58.2 percent from FY2015 to FY2024 in nominal terms. Adjusted for inflation using the GDP deflator, real growth over the same period was approximately 22.4 percent.

SECTION 8: FY2025 AND FY2026 PROJECTIONS

The President's Budget Request submitted to Congress on March 11, 2024 proposed $849.8 billion for national defense in FY2025. However, the continuing resolution environment and subsequent congressional negotiations resulted in a final FY2025 enacted level of $895.2 billion, signed into law on March 8, 2025. This figure includes $13.4 billion in supplemental funding for Indo-Pacific security initiatives.

The Department's Future Years Defense Program (FYDP) projects defense spending to reach $912.0 billion in FY2026, contingent on economic conditions and congressional appropriations action. The FYDP assumes an average annual growth rate of 1.5 percent above inflation through FY2029.

SECTION 9: AUDIT READINESS AND FINANCIAL MANAGEMENT

The Department of Defense has now completed seven consecutive annual financial statement audits, beginning with FY2018. The FY2024 audit, conducted by KPMG LLP and seven other independent public accounting firms, resulted in a disclaimer of opinion for the Department as a whole, consistent with prior years. However, the Defense Logistics Agency and the Defense Health Agency each received qualified opinions, representing improvement over prior year results.

The Department identified 28 material weaknesses in FY2024, down from 35 in FY2022, indicating measurable progress toward achieving a clean audit opinion. The Deputy Secretary of Defense Kathleen Hicks directed all components to achieve clean opinions by FY2028 in a memorandum dated January 9, 2024.

CONCLUSION

FY2024 defense spending of $886.4 billion reflects the Department's sustained effort to modernize the force, address personnel compensation gaps, and compete effectively with strategic rivals in the Indo-Pacific and European theaters. The 4.7 percent nominal increase over FY2023 maintained real purchasing power while prioritizing RDT&E investment at historically high levels. Continued progress on financial audit readiness and procurement efficiency will be essential to ensuring that these resources translate into sustained military advantage.
