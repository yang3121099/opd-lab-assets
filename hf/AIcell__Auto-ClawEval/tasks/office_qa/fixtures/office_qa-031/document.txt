OFFICE OF MANAGEMENT AND BUDGET
FISCAL YEAR 2024 DEFENSE SPENDING ANALYSIS
Prepared by the Budget Analysis Division | Released March 15, 2025

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The Department of Defense received a total appropriation of $886.3 billion for Fiscal Year 2024, representing a 3.2 percent increase over the FY2023 enacted level of $858.5 billion. This report provides a comprehensive breakdown of defense expenditures across major categories, compares current spending to historical benchmarks, and evaluates cost efficiency across the military services. The analysis draws on data submitted by the Office of the Secretary of Defense Comptroller, the Congressional Budget Office's January 2025 Budget and Economic Outlook, and audited financial statements from each military department.

Defense spending in FY2024 represented approximately 13.1 percent of total federal outlays and 3.1 percent of gross domestic product, consistent with the NATO target of 2 percent of GDP that the United States has exceeded continuously since 1960. The base defense budget accounted for $849.8 billion, with an additional $36.5 billion allocated through Overseas Contingency Operations and emergency supplemental funding, primarily directed toward security assistance to Ukraine and Indo-Pacific deterrence initiatives.

SECTION 1: APPROPRIATIONS BY MILITARY DEPARTMENT

The FY2024 National Defense Authorization Act, signed into law by President Biden on December 22, 2023, authorized $886.3 billion in total defense-related spending. The breakdown by department is as follows:

Department of the Army: $185.4 billion
Department of the Navy (including Marine Corps): $230.7 billion
Department of the Air Force (including Space Force): $258.9 billion
Defense-Wide Agencies and Activities: $171.3 billion
Military Construction and Family Housing: $18.6 billion
Defense Emergency Response Fund and Supplemental: $21.4 billion

The Air Force and Space Force combined appropriation of $258.9 billion represents the largest single-department allocation, a position it has held since FY2020 when it surpassed the Navy. The Space Force component alone received $29.4 billion, an increase of 18.7 percent from its FY2023 allocation of $24.8 billion, reflecting accelerated investment in satellite communications, missile warning systems, and space domain awareness capabilities.

Military Personnel costs of $182.7 billion reflect a 5.2 percent pay raise for all uniformed service members effective January 1, 2024, the largest single-year military pay increase since 2002. This raise was intended to address recruiting shortfalls that saw the Army miss its FY2023 enlisted recruiting goal by approximately 10,000 soldiers, falling short of its 65,000-recruit target.

Operations and Maintenance at $310.4 billion remains the single largest spending category, comprising 35.0 percent of total defense outlays. Within this category, $47.3 billion was allocated to depot maintenance and equipment readiness, while $38.6 billion supported base operations and sustainment.

SECTION 3: MAJOR PROCUREMENT PROGRAMS

The $170.3 billion procurement budget funded continuation of several high-cost acquisition programs. The following programs represented the largest individual line items:

F-35 Joint Strike Fighter Program: $15.6 billion for procurement of 83 aircraft across all three variants (F-35A, F-35B, F-35C), managed by Lockheed Martin's Fort Worth, Texas facility. The program's total acquisition cost estimate as of December 2024 stands at $398.6 billion, making it the most expensive weapons system in U.S. history.

Virginia-Class Submarine Program: $8.4 billion for two boats in FY2024, with General Dynamics Electric Boat and Huntington Ingalls Industries sharing construction responsibilities. The Navy's goal of delivering two submarines per year has been hampered by workforce shortages, with FY2023 deliveries totaling only one vessel.

B-21 Raider Bomber: $5.1 billion in FY2024, with Northrop Grumman conducting flight testing at Edwards Air Force Base, California. The Air Force plans to procure a minimum of 100 aircraft at an estimated unit cost of $692 million in then-year dollars.

DDG-51 Arleigh Burke-Class Destroyer: $4.6 billion for two destroyers, with construction split between Bath Iron Works in Bath, Maine, and Huntington Ingalls Industries in Pascagoula, Mississippi.

Guided Multiple Launch Rocket System (GMLRS) and HIMARS Expansion: $2.8 billion, reflecting sustained demand driven by Ukraine security assistance and replenishment of U.S. stockpiles drawn down since February 2022.

SECTION 4: RESEARCH, DEVELOPMENT, TEST AND EVALUATION

The RDT&E budget of $145.9 billion reflects the Department's continued emphasis on next-generation capabilities. Key investment areas include:

Artificial Intelligence and Autonomous Systems: $3.7 billion across 47 separate program elements, coordinated through the Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Office established in February 2022 under Dr. Craig Martell.

Hypersonic Weapons Development: $4.7 billion distributed among Army Long-Range Hypersonic Weapon, Navy Conventional Prompt Strike, and Air Force Hypersonic Attack Cruise Missile programs. The Army's program achieved a successful flight test on March 19, 2024, from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station.

Directed Energy: $1.2 billion for high-energy laser and high-power microwave programs, including the Navy's Optical Dazzling Interdictor and the Army's High Energy Laser Tactical Vehicle Demonstrator.

Nuclear Modernization (RDT&E Component): $8.9 billion supporting the Columbia-class ballistic missile submarine, B-21 Raider, and Sentinel intercontinental ballistic missile programs. The Sentinel program, managed by Northrop Grumman, experienced a Nunn-McCurdy critical breach in January 2024 when its unit cost estimate increased by 37 percent over the original baseline.

SECTION 5: UKRAINE SECURITY ASSISTANCE AND SUPPLEMENTAL FUNDING

Since February 24, 2022, the United States has committed approximately $113.4 billion in total assistance to Ukraine through the end of FY2024, of which $67.2 billion represents security assistance including weapons, equipment, and training. In FY2024 specifically, $23.2 billion was appropriated through the Emergency National Security Supplemental Appropriations Act signed April 24, 2024, of which $13.8 billion was designated for Ukraine military assistance and $9.4 billion for Indo-Pacific security initiatives.

Presidential Drawdown Authority, which permits the President to transfer defense articles from existing U.S. stocks without congressional approval up to a statutory limit, was used 57 times between February 2022 and September 2024, transferring equipment valued at $24.9 billion.

SECTION 6: HISTORICAL COMPARISON AND TREND ANALYSIS

In inflation-adjusted (constant FY2024) dollars, the FY2024 defense budget of $886.3 billion compares to the following historical peaks:

FY2010 (post-surge peak): $858.2 billion
FY1985 (Reagan buildup peak): $712.4 billion
FY1968 (Vietnam War peak): $601.3 billion
FY1953 (Korean War peak): $521.8 billion

Defense spending as a share of GDP has declined substantially from Cold War levels. In FY1962, defense consumed 9.3 percent of GDP. The current 3.1 percent figure, while above the NATO 2 percent guideline, represents a historically low burden relative to the size of the overall economy.

The five-year compound annual growth rate for defense spending from FY2019 ($738.0 billion) to FY2024 ($886.3 billion) is 3.7 percent in nominal terms, which slightly exceeds the average annual inflation rate of 3.4 percent over the same period, indicating modest real growth.

SECTION 7: AUDIT FINDINGS AND FINANCIAL MANAGEMENT

The Department of Defense has failed to achieve a clean audit opinion for the sixth consecutive year. The FY2024 audit, conducted by independent public accounting firms including Ernst & Young, Deloitte, and KPMG under contract to the DoD Inspector General, resulted in a disclaimer of opinion for the Department as a whole. However, four components achieved clean opinions in FY2024: the Defense Finance and Accounting Service, the Defense Contract Audit Agency, the Defense Health Agency, and the Marine Corps.

The DoD Comptroller, Michael McCord, testified before the Senate Armed Services Committee on November 14, 2024, that the Department had corrected 1,126 of 2,847 material weaknesses identified since the audit program began in FY2018, representing a 39.6 percent resolution rate.

CONCLUSION AND OUTLOOK

The FY2025 President's Budget Request, submitted February 10, 2025, proposes $849.8 billion in base defense spending plus anticipated supplemental requests, for a projected total of approximately $895.2 billion, a 1.0 percent nominal increase over FY2024. Congressional action on the FY2025 budget remains pending as of the date of this report, with the Department operating under a continuing resolution at FY2024 enacted levels.

Significant cost pressures in the outyears include nuclear modernization programs projected to cost $756 billion over the next decade according to the Congressional Budget Office's October 2024 estimate, shipbuilding industrial base constraints, and continued inflation in labor and materials costs affecting major acquisition programs.
