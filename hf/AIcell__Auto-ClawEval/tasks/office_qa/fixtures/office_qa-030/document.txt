EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report presents a comprehensive analysis of federal government expenditure patterns across the five-year period from fiscal year 2019 through fiscal year 2024. Total federal outlays grew from $4.45 trillion in FY2019 to $6.13 trillion in FY2024, representing a cumulative increase of 37.8 percent over the review period. The analysis identifies key drivers of expenditure growth, examines departmental spending trajectories, and highlights areas of fiscal concern requiring legislative attention in the upcoming budget cycle.

The COVID-19 pandemic produced the most significant single-year expenditure spike in the review period, with FY2021 outlays reaching $6.82 trillion before declining as emergency relief programs concluded. Mandatory spending programs, particularly Social Security and Medicare, continue to represent the largest and fastest-growing components of the federal budget.

SECTION 1: AGGREGATE EXPENDITURE OVERVIEW

Total federal outlays by fiscal year are summarized in the table below.

FEDERAL OUTLAYS FY2019–FY2024

Fiscal Year | Total Outlays | Year-over-Year Change | GDP Percentage
FY2019      | $4.45 trillion | —                    | 20.7%
FY2020      | $6.55 trillion | +47.2%               | 31.2%
FY2021      | $6.82 trillion | +4.1%                | 30.5%
FY2022      | $6.27 trillion | -8.1%                | 25.1%
FY2023      | $6.13 trillion | -2.2%                | 22.7%
FY2024      | $6.13 trillion | 0.0%                | 21.9%

The extraordinary jump between FY2019 and FY2020 reflects the enactment of the Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security (CARES) Act on March 27, 2020, which authorized $2.2 trillion in emergency spending. The American Rescue Plan Act of March 2021 contributed an additional $1.9 trillion in outlays, sustaining elevated spending into FY2021.

Excluding emergency supplemental appropriations, baseline discretionary spending grew at an average annual rate of 4.3 percent across the review period, consistent with historical norms.

SECTION 2: MANDATORY SPENDING ANALYSIS

Mandatory spending programs accounted for 63.4 percent of total federal outlays in FY2024, up from 61.1 percent in FY2019. The three largest mandatory programs are Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid.

SOCIAL SECURITY
Social Security outlays totaled $1.35 trillion in FY2024, compared to $1.04 trillion in FY2019, representing a 29.8 percent increase over five years. The average annual growth rate of 5.4 percent reflects both cost-of-living adjustments and the continued retirement of the Baby Boomer generation. The Social Security Administration reported 71.3 million beneficiaries as of December 2023, up from 64.2 million in December 2018.

MEDICARE
Medicare expenditures reached $1.02 trillion in FY2024, crossing the one-trillion-dollar threshold for the first time in program history. This compares to $799 billion in FY2019, a 27.7 percent increase. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) attributes approximately 18 percent of this growth to the expansion of Medicare Advantage enrollment, which reached 32.8 million beneficiaries in 2024.

MEDICAID
Medicaid outlays totaled $616 billion in FY2024, down from a pandemic peak of $734 billion in FY2022. The decline reflects the conclusion of continuous enrollment provisions established under the Families First Coronavirus Response Act, which expired on March 31, 2023. Approximately 8.2 million individuals were disenrolled from Medicaid between April 2023 and December 2023 during the "unwinding" process.

SECTION 3: DISCRETIONARY SPENDING ANALYSIS

Total discretionary spending in FY2024 was $1.74 trillion, representing 28.4 percent of total outlays. Defense discretionary spending accounted for $886 billion, or 50.9 percent of all discretionary outlays.

DEFENSE SPENDING
The Department of Defense base budget grew from $686 billion in FY2019 to $886 billion in FY2024, an increase of $200 billion or 29.2 percent. The FY2024 National Defense Authorization Act, signed December 22, 2023, authorized the largest single-year defense budget increase since FY2003, adding $28 billion above the FY2023 enacted level. Personnel costs represent the single largest component of defense spending at $182 billion, followed by operations and maintenance at $274 billion.

NON-DEFENSE DISCRETIONARY SPENDING
Non-defense discretionary (NDD) spending totaled $854 billion in FY2024. Key departmental allocations include:

- Department of Education: $79.6 billion (up 12.4% from FY2019's $70.8 billion)
- Department of Transportation: $105.3 billion (up 61.2% from FY2019's $65.3 billion, reflecting Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act appropriations)
- Department of Veterans Affairs: $135.2 billion (up 47.8% from FY2019's $91.5 billion)
- Department of Health and Human Services (discretionary): $119.8 billion
- Department of Housing and Urban Development: $73.4 billion

The Department of Transportation increase is the most significant in percentage terms among major agencies, driven by the $550 billion Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act signed November 15, 2021, which allocated $110 billion specifically for roads and bridges over five years.

SECTION 4: INTEREST ON THE NATIONAL DEBT

Net interest payments on the federal debt represented one of the fastest-growing expenditure categories in the review period. Interest outlays totaled $659 billion in FY2024, compared to $375 billion in FY2019, an increase of 75.7 percent in five years. As a share of total outlays, net interest rose from 8.4 percent in FY2019 to 10.7 percent in FY2024.

The Federal Reserve's rate-hiking cycle, which began in March 2022 and raised the federal funds rate from near zero to a target range of 5.25–5.50 percent by July 2023, substantially increased the government's borrowing costs. The average interest rate on outstanding Treasury securities rose from 1.9 percent in FY2021 to 3.1 percent in FY2024. The Congressional Budget Office projects net interest payments will reach $870 billion by FY2026 if current rate conditions persist.

SECTION 5: EXPENDITURE AS A SHARE OF GDP

Federal spending as a percentage of gross domestic product provides a normalized measure of fiscal scale. The FY2020 ratio of 31.2 percent was the highest recorded since World War II, surpassing the previous post-war peak of 25.2 percent reached in FY2009 during the financial crisis response. By FY2024, the ratio had declined to 21.9 percent, approaching the 40-year historical average of approximately 21.0 percent.

The Office of Management and Budget projects the ratio will remain between 22.1 and 23.4 percent through FY2030, driven primarily by mandatory spending growth exceeding nominal GDP growth.

SECTION 6: AREAS OF FISCAL CONCERN

The following areas have been flagged by the Office of Budget Management for heightened monitoring:

1. INTEREST COST TRAJECTORY
At $659 billion, net interest now exceeds total non-defense discretionary spending in several major categories. If the debt-to-GDP ratio continues rising from its current level of 97.3 percent, interest costs could crowd out productive investments in infrastructure, education, and research.

2. SOCIAL SECURITY TRUST FUND DEPLETION
The Social Security Board of Trustees 2023 Annual Report projects the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance Trust Fund will be depleted by 2033, at which point incoming revenues would cover only 77 percent of scheduled benefits. Without legislative action, this represents a structural expenditure risk of approximately $350 billion annually.

3. VETERANS HEALTH ADMINISTRATION COST GROWTH
VA healthcare costs have grown 52.3 percent since FY2019, partly due to the PACT Act signed August 10, 2022, which expanded eligibility for veterans exposed to toxic substances. CMS estimates an additional 3.5 million veterans will become eligible for VA healthcare over the next decade, with associated cost implications of $280 billion over ten years.

SECTION 7: CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

The five-year expenditure trend reveals a federal budget under structural pressure from three converging forces: aging demographics driving mandatory program growth, elevated interest costs resulting from pandemic-era debt accumulation, and expanded statutory obligations in veterans' healthcare and infrastructure.

The Office of Budget Management recommends the following actions for consideration in the FY2025 budget process:

- Establish a bipartisan Social Security solvency commission with a reporting deadline of December 31, 2025
- Conduct a comprehensive program integrity review of Medicaid re-enrollment following the unwinding process, targeting an estimated $47 billion in improper payments identified by the Government Accountability Office in its January 2024 report
- Accelerate Treasury debt management strategies to extend the average maturity of outstanding securities, currently at 6.1 years, to reduce refinancing risk
- Require all cabinet departments with year-over-year spending increases exceeding 8 percent to submit supplemental justification reports to the Appropriations Committees within 60 days of enactment
