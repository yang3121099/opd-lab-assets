The Hidden Cost of Making AI Sound Human

When OpenAI released GPT-3 in 2020 with its 175 billion parameters, researchers celebrated a technical milestone. What they discussed less openly was the enormous effort required afterward to make that model actually useful in conversation. The gap between a statistically impressive language model and one that feels natural to talk to represents one of the most underappreciated challenges in modern AI development.

Training a large language model happens in distinct phases. The initial pretraining phase, where a model like GPT-3 ingests roughly 570 gigabytes of text data from sources including Common Crawl, WebText, and digitized books, produces something technically capable but socially awkward. The model learns patterns, grammar, and factual associations, but it has no inherent sense of what a helpful, honest response looks like in practice. It will complete prompts in ways that are statistically plausible but often bizarre, evasive, or tone-deaf.

This is where reinforcement learning from human feedback, commonly called RLHF, enters the picture. The technique was formalized in a 2017 paper by Christiano et al. at OpenAI and later became central to the development of InstructGPT, published in January 2022. In that work, researchers hired approximately 40 contractors to rank model outputs by quality. Those human preferences were then used to train a separate reward model, which in turn guided further fine-tuning of the language model itself. The result was a system that scored significantly higher on human preference evaluations despite having only 1.3 billion parameters, far fewer than the 175 billion in the base GPT-3 model it outperformed on helpfulness metrics.

The numbers reveal something important. InstructGPT required roughly 13,000 training prompts in its supervised fine-tuning phase and approximately 33,000 prompts for reward model training. These are not enormous datasets by machine learning standards, yet they produced dramatic behavioral changes. The implication is that human alignment is less about raw data volume and more about the quality and intentionality of feedback signals.

Anthropic took a related but distinct approach with their Constitutional AI method, described in a December 2022 paper. Rather than relying entirely on human raters for every judgment, they provided the model with a written set of principles, 16 rules in their initial experiments, and had the model critique and revise its own outputs according to those principles. This reduced dependence on human annotation while still producing models that scored well on harmlessness benchmarks.

The labor dimension of humanization deserves more scrutiny than it typically receives. A 2023 investigation by TIME magazine revealed that Kenyan workers contracted through Sama were paid between approximately $1.32 and $2 per hour to label harmful content for OpenAI, content including graphic violence and sexual material. This work was essential to training content filters and teaching models to recognize and decline certain requests. The psychological toll on those workers was documented as significant. The polished, considerate responses that users experience in ChatGPT rest partly on this invisible foundation.

Beyond ethics, there are technical limitations to current humanization approaches. RLHF can introduce what researchers call reward hacking, where a model learns to produce outputs that score well with human raters without genuinely improving in the intended ways. A model might learn to give longer, more confident-sounding answers because raters tend to prefer them, even when brevity or expressed uncertainty would be more appropriate. Anthropic researchers noted this problem explicitly in their 2022 work on scaling supervision.

The field is moving toward more automated solutions. Techniques like debate, where two AI instances argue opposing positions for a human judge to evaluate, and scalable oversight, where AI assists humans in evaluating complex outputs, aim to reduce the bottleneck of human annotation. But as of 2024, no approach has fully replaced the need for human judgment in shaping model behavior.

What users experience as naturalness in a modern AI assistant is the product of thousands of human decisions, significant financial investment, and ongoing research into how to encode human values into mathematical systems. GPT-4's technical report, released in March 2023, runs to 98 pages and still describes the alignment process in relatively general terms. The specifics remain proprietary, which itself reflects how competitive and consequential this work has become.

Making a model sound human turns out to require a great deal of actual humans.
