The Hidden Cost of Making AI Sound Human

When OpenAI released GPT-3 in 2020 with its 175 billion parameters, researchers celebrated a milestone in language generation. The model could write coherent paragraphs, answer questions, and even produce passable poetry. But engineers quickly noticed something uncomfortable: the outputs felt mechanical. Sentences were technically correct but rhythmically flat, like reading a legal brief written by someone who had never spoken aloud.

This observation launched an entire subfield of AI development focused on what practitioners now call humanization, the process of training models to produce text that feels natural, varied, and contextually aware in the way human writing actually is.

The technical challenge is more specific than it sounds. Standard language models are trained to predict the next token in a sequence by minimizing cross-entropy loss across enormous datasets. The result is statistically average text. When a model has seen the phrase "it is important to note" approximately 4.2 million times in its training corpus, it reaches for that phrase constantly, because statistically it fits. Human writers, by contrast, avoid their own repetitions instinctively. We get bored with ourselves.

One of the primary techniques used to address this is Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback, or RLHF, which Anthropic and OpenAI both deployed extensively between 2021 and 2023. In this approach, human raters evaluate model outputs and rank them by preference. Those rankings train a separate reward model, which then guides the language model toward outputs humans find more natural and useful. The process is expensive. Training a single RLHF cycle for a large model can require thousands of hours of human annotation labor, with annotator teams sometimes numbering in the hundreds.

But RLHF alone does not solve the humanization problem. It tends to make models more agreeable and safer, not necessarily more human-sounding. Researchers at Google DeepMind published findings in 2023 showing that RLHF-trained models scored higher on helpfulness metrics but showed increased rates of what they called "formulaic hedging," phrases like "certainly" and "of course" appearing at rates three to four times higher than in comparable human writing samples.

A different approach involves fine-tuning on curated human writing rather than the broad internet. Models trained on literary fiction, personal essays, and journalism show measurably different output characteristics. Perplexity scores, which measure how surprised a model is by the next word in a sequence, drop significantly when models are exposed to stylistically diverse human prose. Lower perplexity in this context suggests the model has internalized more varied sentence structures and vocabulary patterns.

Some teams have experimented with what they call contrastive training, where the model is shown pairs of outputs, one generated by an earlier model version and one written by a human, and trained to identify and replicate the differences. Early results from a 2022 Stanford NLP group paper suggested this method reduced detectable AI patterns by roughly 34 percent in blind human evaluations, though the sample sizes were modest at around 800 evaluated passages.

The irony embedded in all of this work is worth sitting with. We are training machines to hide the fact that they are machines. The goal is not accuracy or truthfulness in any deep sense but mimicry of a particular aesthetic. Human writing is full of errors, digressions, and idiosyncratic choices. A sentence fragment used for emphasis. Starting a sentence with "And" when the grammar books say not to. These are not bugs in human communication. They are features that signal authenticity, and models trained purely on correctness will never reproduce them naturally.

There is also a measurement problem that the field has not resolved cleanly. How do you quantify "sounds human"? Current benchmarks rely on human evaluators, automated detectors like GPTZero or Originality.ai, and statistical measures like burstiness scores, which track whether sentence length varies in the irregular patterns typical of human writing. Each metric captures something real but incomplete.

What is clear is that the gap between technically correct and genuinely natural is not closing as fast as the raw capability numbers suggest. A model with 70 billion parameters can pass a bar exam but still write a condolence card that feels hollow. Closing that gap is less a matter of scale and more a matter of understanding what human language is actually doing when it works, which turns out to be a harder question than anyone expected.
